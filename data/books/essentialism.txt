Essentialism — Study Notes
Author of original book: Greg McKeown
Topic: Prioritization and the disciplined pursuit of less

CORE THESIS

Essentialism is the disciplined pursuit of less, but better. McKeown's diagnosis: the undisciplined pursuit of more — saying yes to nearly everything — spreads effort a millimeter deep in a million directions. Success itself breeds the problem: doing well creates options and demands, which erode the focus that created success (the "paradox of success"). The Essentialist inverts the default: instead of asking "How can I fit it all in?", ask "What is the one thing I can do that matters most?" If you don't prioritize your life, someone else will.

THE ESSENTIALIST MINDSET (ESSENCE)

Three core realizations: (1) Choice — "I choose to" replaces "I have to"; the ability to choose cannot be taken away, only forgotten. (2) Discernment — almost everything is noise; a few things are vastly more valuable (the power law hiding behind effort). "Almost everything is nonessential." (3) Trade-offs — you cannot do it all; the question is not "How can I do both?" but "Which problem do I want?" Straddling strategies fail; real strategy requires choosing what not to do.

EXPLORE: DISCERN THE VITAL FEW

Paradoxically, Essentialists explore more options than Nonessentialists — but commit to fewer. Tools: create space to escape and think (blocked time for reading and reflection); keep a journal to see patterns; play, which broadens thinking; and protect sleep, which multiplies effectiveness rather than stealing from it. The selection criterion is extreme: apply the 90 Percent Rule — score an option, and if it is not a clear 90+ out of 100, treat it as a zero. "If it isn't a clear yes, it's a clear no." Vague, broad criteria admit everything; explicit, selective criteria filter almost everything.

ELIMINATE: CUT THE TRIVIAL MANY

- Clarify intent: an "essential intent" is one decision that eliminates a thousand later decisions (concrete and inspirational at once).
- Dare to say no: a graceful no earns respect in the long run; a resentful yes costs it. Separate the decision from the relationship — you are refusing a request, not a person.
- Uncommit: sunk-cost bias keeps us pouring effort into yesterday's commitments. Ask the zero-based question: "If I weren't already invested in this, what would I do to get in?" If nothing, stop.
- Edit: like a film editor, cut ruthlessly so the essential has room; editing life is subtraction, not addition.
- Set boundaries: fences protect your priorities; other people's problems are not automatically your problems. Boundaries are liberating, not limiting.

EXECUTE: MAKE THE ESSENTIAL EFFORTLESS

- Buffer: build in margin — plan for the unexpected, add 50 percent to time estimates, prepare early.
- Subtract obstacles: identify the one constraint slowing the system and remove it (rather than adding more force).
- Progress: small wins compounded beat heroic pushes; celebrate incremental advances.
- Routine: design routines that enshrine the essential, so execution becomes the default rather than a daily battle.
- Focus on the now: you can only ever act in the present; figure out what matters right now.

ACTIONABLE PRACTICES

- Write an essential intent for the quarter: one measurable, meaningful objective.
- Apply the 90 Percent Rule to every new request for two weeks.
- Schedule a weekly "explore" block: no meetings, only thinking/reading.
- Choose one existing commitment that fails the zero-based test and exit it gracefully.

CONNECTIONS TO OTHER BOOKS

Essentialism generalizes The One Thing's focusing question to whole-life design. Deep Work supplies the execution mechanics for the chosen essential. The Subtle Art makes the same argument in the register of caring; Effortless (McKeown's sequel) and The Art of Laziness extend the make-it-easy principle.
